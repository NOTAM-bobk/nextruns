Under-fueling is one of the most common, most preventable problems in distance running, and it's almost always a training and habit problem rather than a willpower problem — most runners simply haven't practiced eating while running enough for it to feel routine on race day.

## The baseline number

For runs over about 90 minutes, a reasonable starting target is 30–60g of carbohydrate per hour, working up toward 60–90g/hour for well-trained runners who've built up gut tolerance over time. That's roughly one to two standard energy gels an hour, or the equivalent in chews, sports drink, or real food.

Runs under 60–75 minutes generally don't need in-run fueling at all if you're eating normally the rest of the day — save the gels for when they're actually solving a problem.

## Train your gut like you train your legs

Race-day nausea from fueling is usually a training gap, not a fragile stomach. If you've never practiced taking in 60g of carbohydrate an hour during a long run, race day — with its adrenaline, heat, and unfamiliar pace — is the worst possible time to find out your gut isn't ready for it. Practice your exact race-day fueling plan on at least three or four long runs before it matters.

## Hydration is about more than water

Sweat carries sodium, and a long run in the heat with only plain water can leave you diluting what electrolytes you have left. If you're a heavy or salty sweater — visible white residue on dark clothing is a decent clue — an electrolyte mix or salt tabs during long efforts are worth experimenting with, again well before race day.

## A simple long-run fueling framework

- **Before:** a carbohydrate-focused meal 2–3 hours out, small enough to fully digest.
- **During (90+ min runs):** 30–60g carbohydrate per hour, starting around 30–45 minutes in rather than waiting until you feel low — by the time you feel it, you're already behind.
- **After:** carbohydrate and protein within the next couple of hours, roughly a 3:1 or 4:1 carb-to-protein ratio, to kick off glycogen refilling and muscle repair.

## Common mistakes

- **Waiting too long to start fueling.** Once you feel the bonk coming, it's harder to recover from than to have prevented.
- **Trying something new on race day.** New gel flavor, new sports drink, new breakfast — race morning is not the time to experiment.
- **Under-fueling because of body composition goals.** A marathon block is a bad time to also be running a calorie deficit; the two goals compete directly, and under-fueled training blocks show up as stalled workouts and slow recovery.

None of this needs to be complicated. Pick a fueling plan, rehearse it on your long runs until it's boring, and boring is exactly what you want it to be by the time it matters.
