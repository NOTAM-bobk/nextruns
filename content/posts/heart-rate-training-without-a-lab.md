The usual advice is to compute your zones from a formula — 220 minus age, or one of its fancier descendants — and then wonder why zone 3 feels like zone 2 and zone 2 feels like a warm-up. The formulas are population averages. You are not a population. I've tested runners of the same age whose max heart rates were 34 bpm apart, which is the difference between their entire easy and threshold zones.

## Get a real max heart rate

You have three realistic options, in ascending order of unpleasantness:

1. **Race something short.** A 5K finished at full effort, with a sprint for the line, gets most people within a few beats of true max. Look at the peak value, not the average.
2. **Hill repeats.** Find a hill that takes 2–3 minutes hard, run 4–5 of them with a jog back down, and go all-out on the last one. Almost as good, and less public.
3. **A lab test.** Accurate, expensive, and you'll get a lactate profile thrown in. Worth it once if you're serious about long-term training; not required.

Whatever number you get, subtract nothing and add nothing. If your watch says 187 at the top of that final hill, your max is 187 until a future test says otherwise.

## Then calibrate the bottom

Max heart rate alone doesn't finish the job — you also need a honest resting value, because your useful zones live between the two. Take your heart rate for a full minute before getting out of bed, three or four mornings in a row, and use the lowest. A runner with a max of 187 and a resting rate of 38 is training with completely different zones than a runner with the same max and a resting rate of 65, even though every formula would hand them the same numbers.

The simple field method that works better than any formula: **threshold is the hardest effort you could hold for roughly an hour.** Do a 30-minute solo time trial at that effort, wear the monitor, and average the last 20 minutes. That's your threshold heart rate. Zone 2 sits about 15–25 beats below it for most runners; easy running should live there or below.

## The part nobody warns you about

Heart rate drifts. On a hot day, your pace at a given heart rate can drop 20–30 seconds per kilometer, and that's normal physiology, not a fitness collapse. It drifts upward during long runs (cardiac drift), lags behind effort on intervals, and reads high when you're dehydrated, sick, or underslept. This is why the runners who get the most out of heart-rate training treat it as a *constraint on easy days* — keep easy runs under the cap — and run workouts by pace or feel, where heart rate is a breadcrumb trail rather than a steering wheel.

## One month of patience

If you've been gray-zone running for years, your new zone 2 will feel insulting. You'll be jogging at paces you'd normally warm up past. Give it a month before judging: within three to four weeks, the same heart rate produces noticeably faster paces, and the easy days stop feeling like a waste because the hard days start going better. That trade — slower Tuesdays for faster Saturdays — is the entire deal.
