The strength-training conversation for runners oscillates between two bad extremes: a 45-minute glute-band circuit that mostly trains patience, and a powerlifting program that wrecks your Tuesday workout. The evidence sits in a narrower, simpler place.

## What strength work actually buys a runner

The highest-quality evidence points to three things: **economy** (heavy strength training measurably reduces the energy cost of running at a given pace), **injury resilience** (stronger tissue tolerates more load — the mechanism behind most successful tendon-rehab protocols is progressive heavy loading), and **late-race form** (the back half of a marathon is partly a strength test). Notably absent from the strong-evidence list: most "activation" exercises, and any claim that lifting will transform your VO2max.

## The two-lift core

If you only do two movements, make them a **squat or leg press** and a **hinge** (deadlift or Romanian deadlift). Together they cover the main propulsive and load-absorbing chains. Add a calf raise — the Achilles takes more cumulative load in running than any other structure — and one upper-body pull for balance. That's four exercises. Two sessions a week, 30–40 minutes each, is the effective dose in most of the research.

## Heavy, not burning

The counterintuitive part: the economy and tendon benefits come from **heavy, low-rep lifting** — something like 3–4 sets of 3–6 reps at a load you could lift maybe 8 times — not from high-rep burnout sets. Heavy loads recruit the high-threshold motor units running rarely touches and load tendon in the range that stimulates adaptation. The burn of a 20-rep set feels more runner-ish and does less for the goals above. Start lighter than you think for two weeks, learn the movements, then progress the load slowly and relentlessly.

## Scheduling without sabotage

Strength work costs recovery, so place it where the running cost is lowest: after a quality run rather than the day before it, or on the same day with at least 6 hours between sessions. During a race block, cut to one maintenance session a week and drop it entirely in the final 10 days — the last thing a taper needs is fresh squats. In base season, be ambitious; in peak season, be minimal but present.

## The honest failure mode

Most runners don't fail at strength training by choosing wrong exercises. They fail by quitting in week six when running gets busy, then restarting from scratch three times a year. A modest program sustained for years beats an optimal program sustained for two months. Pick the smallest version you'll actually keep — even one session, two lifts, 25 minutes — and let consistency do the compounding.
