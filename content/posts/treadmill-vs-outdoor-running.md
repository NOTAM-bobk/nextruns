Every winter the same question comes back: does treadmill running "count"? Short answer — almost everything transfers, and the two differences that do exist are manageable once you know them.

## The belt helps, slightly

A moving belt removes a little of the work your hamstrings do to propel you forward, and there's no air resistance at any speed. Lab comparisons put the difference at roughly 1–4% energy cost at typical easy-run speeds — small, but real. The common compensation is to set the grade to 1%, which roughly cancels the effect at paces around 5:00/km or slower. Faster than that and 1% slightly overcorrects; slower and it's more than enough. Either way, this is a rounding error in your training week, not a scandal.

Where it matters more: pacing precision. The belt holds an exact speed in a way roads never do, which makes the treadmill genuinely *better* for tempo runs and cruise intervals when you're learning what a given effort feels like. Just recalibrate expectations outdoors afterward — your treadmill tempo pace will feel about 10–15 seconds per kilometer harder on the road at the same heart rate.

## Downhill doesn't exist

This is the difference that bites people. Motorized treadmills top out around –3% grade, and many runners do most of their winter miles at 0%. Quads take eccentric pounding on downhills that flat running never reproduces, and the first hilly spring long run after a flat treadmill winter is a classic quad-soreness factory. If your target race has any descent worth naming, find even a gentle outdoor rolling loop once a week, or accept a sore week in March.

## The missing world

The other difference isn't biomechanical. Outdoor running asks you to navigate, regulate effort across wind and terrain and crossings, and stay out for the whole run with no pause button twenty steps from your front door. Treadmill runs end the moment they get boring. If your long runs keep mysteriously shrinking on the belt, that's not weakness — it's the machine doing exactly what it's designed to do. Put the long run outside whenever conditions allow, and use the treadmill for the midweek runs where convenience is the whole point.

## What I'd actually tell a friend

Use the treadmill without guilt for easy runs, bad weather, and controlled workouts. Keep one outdoor long run a week when you can. Add downhill or uneven ground somewhere in the week if your goal race involves it. And calibrate treadmill pace against outdoor pace a few times a season so the numbers in your log mean the same thing year-round.
