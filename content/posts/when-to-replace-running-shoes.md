The standard advice — replace your shoes every 500 to 800 kilometers — is simultaneously true and nearly useless. The range is so wide because the real variables are you, your routes, and the shoe itself. A 70-kilogram runner landing midfoot on smooth asphalt in a durable trainer can push well past 800. An 85-kilogram heel-striker on broken pavement in a lightweight racer might be done at 400. The counter is a hint; the shoe is the evidence.

## The signs that matter

**The press test, honestly applied.** Midsole foam dies by losing rebound, not by visibly collapsing. Press a thumb into the midsole beside your heel and under the forefoot. Fresh foam pushes back firmly and evenly. Dead foam feels hard, and — this is the part people miss — it feels *the same everywhere*, because it has compressed flat. Compare against the same spot on a new shoe if you can.

**New aches in familiar places.** The most reliable signal most runners get is medical rather than visual: a niggle in the knee or shin that appears around the same point in every shoe's life. If your left knee starts whispering at kilometer 550 in every pair you own, your body is telling you your replacement interval more precisely than any chart.

**The outsole lies; the midsole doesn't.** Worn tread rubber looks alarming and often affects nothing — plenty of shoes run fine with the heel lug smoothed off. Meanwhile a shoe can look nearly new and be functionally dead because the foam inside flattened in week three. Judge the foam, not the paint.

**Lopsided wear.** If one shoe's midsole is visibly more compressed than the other, or you can see the pair leaning on a flat table, replace them even if the mileage seems low. Asymmetric dead foam is how good shoes produce bad knees.

## What actually extends shoe life

Rotate two pairs if you run more than about 50 kilometers a week. Foam decompresses slowly between runs — a shoe that runs every other day consistently outlives two shoes run on alternating days each covering the same total, and rotating also gives you a built-in comparison for the press test. Let wet shoes dry at room temperature, stuffed with newspaper, not on a radiator: heat bakes foam and adhesives. And unlace properly — stomping heels off at the door crushes the heel counter long before its time.

## Keep a spare in the rotation

The one habit worth stealing from serious marathoners: when a favorite model is being discontinued or redesigned, buy the next pair while the current one still has life. New versions of the same shoe are frequently quite different — midsole foams change wholesale — and discovering that mid-training-block is poor timing. Retire shoes from running at the first persistent niggle; they still make perfectly good lawn-mowing and dog-walking shoes, which is a more dignified end than most of us will get.
